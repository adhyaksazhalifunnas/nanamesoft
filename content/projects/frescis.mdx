    alternatives:
      - "A native Android app, which is what Fishku, the closest competitor, had built"
      - "An offline-first progressive web app with a bundled model"
    rationale: >
      The moment of need is standing at a stall having just picked up a fish. An
      app store install is a bigger ask at that moment than the check is worth, and
      it is the one weakness we could name in the competitor's product. A URL opens
      in a second and the camera is available from the browser.
    tradeoff: >
      We gave up reliable camera control, offline use, and the ability to ship the
      model to the device. Everything now requires a connection at the stall, which
      is exactly where a connection is least dependable.
  - decision: "Where to run the model"
    chosen: "Roboflow for hosted inference, called from Python Azure Functions with OpenCV handling the image"
    alternatives:
      - "Self-hosting the trained model on a rented VM or container"
      - "Running inference in the browser with a converted model"
    rationale: >
      A serverless function costs nothing between requests, which suits a product
      with no users yet and a team with no budget. Splitting inference out to
      Roboflow also meant the model could be retrained in the Colab notebook and
      swapped without redeploying the backend — useful when the model was still
      changing weekly.
    tradeoff: >
      Two external dependencies on the critical path, and a cold start on the first
      request after an idle period. We traded predictable latency for zero idle
      cost, which is the right trade for a course project and the wrong one for a
      market stall.
  - decision: "Which database to use for detection history"
    chosen: "MongoDB, with the uploaded images in Cloudinary and only their references stored"
    alternatives:
      - "A relational database matching the ERD we had already designed"
      - "Storing images as blobs alongside the records"
    rationale: >
      We designed an ERD during requirements and then noted in the same document
      that the implementation might not be relational. It was not: a detection
      record is a self-contained document — image reference, prediction, timestamp,
      owner — and nothing about it needs a join. Keeping the image bytes out of the
      database entirely made the records small and the history view cheap to page
      through.
    tradeoff: >
      Two stores to keep consistent instead of one. A record can outlive its image
      or point at one that failed to upload, and nothing in the schema stops that —
      we handle it by checking at read time rather than preventing it at write time.
  - decision: "Which SDLC to run the project under"
    chosen: "Waterfall, with requirements, design and implementation as sequential phases"
    alternatives:
      - "An iterative or agile cycle with a working slice each sprint"
    rationale: >
      The scope was small and well understood, the deadline was a fixed course
      milestone, and a sequential plan made the schedule legible to three people
      working around other classes. For a system with this little complexity, the
      coordination overhead of iteration was harder to justify than the plan.
    tradeoff: >
      The feature list was locked before we knew what the model could actually do
      well. If freshness detection had turned out to work only on certain species,
      or only in certain light, we had no planned point at which to revise what we
      were building around it.

limitations:
  - "Freshness is inferred from the eye alone. It is a real signal and the one a buyer can see without touching the fish, but a fish can have a clear eye and still have been stored badly — the gills and the flesh carry information this app never looks at."
  - "90.4% is the training platform's own figure over our dataset, not a field result. Nobody has measured how the model does on a fish it has never seen, photographed by a stranger, at a stall we did not choose."
  - "The 0.6 confidence gate is a number we picked, not one we tuned. It rejects obvious non-fish images cleanly, but we never measured how often it also refuses a legitimate, slightly blurry photo of an eye — which is the failure the user actually feels."

## Context

Buying fish at a traditional market is a judgement call made in a few seconds,
and most buyers are not equipped to make it. Freshness changes the taste of
everything cooked afterwards, but by the time that is apparent the transaction is
long over.

The information is not hidden. A fresh fish's eye is clear, bright and slightly
domed; as it ages the eye clouds, reddens and sinks. Sellers know this, and
experienced buyers know this. It is learnable — it is just not widely known, and
nobody is going to learn it standing at a stall.

We looked at what already existed. Fishku, the closest competitor, had a
freshness-detection feature and had placed in the top 16 of a national capstone
programme — but it was a native mobile app, consumer and seller versions, with an
install between the user and the answer. The other two we studied, SILLY FISH and
eFresh, were online stores: they sell you fish and assert its freshness, which
means trusting the seller rather than checking. That gap defined the product. We
wanted the check to be available to someone holding a fish that someone else is
selling them, with nothing to install.

The brief we set was three features: predict freshness from a photo of the eye,
keep a history of what you checked, and a general reference section on edible
fish.

## Implementation

The system is three pieces with a deliberately thin seam between them.

**The client** is a Next.js app built mobile-first — the design assumption
throughout was a phone held at arm's length in a market. It uses `react-webcam`
to take the photo in the browser rather than asking for an upload, Ant Design for
the interface so three people could build consistent screens quickly, and
Firebase for Google sign-in.

**The backend** is Python on Azure Functions, and it is small on purpose: two
endpoints. `Predict` receives the image, runs it through OpenCV, calls the hosted
model, stores the image in Cloudinary and the record in MongoDB, and returns the
verdict. `GetHistories` reads that history back for the signed-in user, grouped by
day so the list stays navigable as it grows.

**The model** lives in Roboflow, trained in a Colab notebook on 1,046 fish-eye
photographs. It classifies into three bands rather than a yes/no — highly fresh,
fresh, not fresh — because freshness is a gradient and a binary answer would be
claiming a precision the eye does not give you.

The part I would defend hardest is what happens when the photo is not a fish eye
at all. A prediction under 0.6 confidence does not return the closest class; it
returns "not a fish eye" and asks for another photo. We tested that against a
photo of a fish body, a meme, and a landscape, and all three were refused. A
model that confidently tells someone their doorframe is a fresh mackerel is worse
than one that admits it cannot see. Keeping the model outside
the backend is the decision that made the team work: the model could be retrained
and republished without touching or redeploying the API, which meant model work
and application work did not block each other in a semester where both were
happening at once.

Leading three people through this mostly meant deciding those boundaries early.
We worked the design out in Figma first, agreed the shape of the request and
response between client and function, and then the frontend and the prediction
path could be built in parallel against that contract.

The thing I would change is the split between what runs where. Putting inference
behind two external services was right for a project with no budget and no users,
and it is the wrong architecture for the actual moment of use — a market stall
with patchy signal. A smaller model running on the device would be slower to
build and far better to use.
